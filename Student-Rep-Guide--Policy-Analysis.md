## Background
* Policy is a core function of educational institutions writ-large and within units (e.g., faculties, departments).
* Likewise, policy serves an important function in student organizations (e.g., student unions, clubs, honour societies).
* Student reps are not always prepared for or knowledgeable about policy analysis.
* Policy language and structure are developed meticulously and iteratively.
* This document summarizes foundational knowledge on policy analysis for student representatives.
* Specifically, this doc explains various circumstances in which one might engage with policy and their respective analytic steps.

## Content

There are 3 main circumstances one interacts with a policy on a governance body:
1.	**Analyzing/critiquing existing policy:** First, when there is a cyclical review of the policy in question, and before any specific amendments are proposed, the body is expected to analyze and critique the existing policy. This circumstance serves to generate possibilities and considerations for future amendment drafting.
2.	**Evaluating proposed amendments:** Second, when policy amendments are brought forward to the body for debate and approval.
3.	**Proposing new policies or amendments:** Third, when observant of shortcoming or gap in policy in real time, one may contemplate targeted critiques to advocate for improvements; this may be based on one’s constituency within the organization or community.

In order to formally critique policy, there are 3 stages:
1. Analysis (and sometimes design)
2. Evaluation
3. Articulation.
When proposing your own policy or amendment, creation/design of the policy/amendment is part of the analysis stage.

1. **Analysis:** During the process of reading the policy, one must analyze the policy by breaking it into its components. There are 2 types of components: (a) the principles of the policy (which may or may not be written in the document itself), and (b) the terms of the policy itself (which may be express or implied). The principles can be subdivided into two components: primary (i.e., non-contentious) and secondary (i.e., contentious) principles. Primary principles refer to those that are unequivocally the core of the organisation’s purpose. Secondary principles refers to ideals that are not central to the purpose in 1 of 3 ways: (i) the ideals may be shared among some in the organisation/community, but not necessarily others; the ideals may be obvious to some in the organisation/community, but less obvious to others in the community and require more substantial dialogue; (iii) the ideals are shared among those in the organisation/community but there is mixed opinion on the extent that they need to be addressed through policy.
2. **Evaluation:** Using the above analysis, one must evaluate the policy, which means making logical judgments about how well the terms of the policy enact the underlying principles. Truly effective evaluation considers the big picture and indirect consequences that may not manifest immediately, but nonetheless have substantial impact (positive or negative) on the upholding of the intended values. 
3. **Articulation:** based on one’s evaluation, one must articulate the critique in a pragmatic manner. This involves constructing a coherent argument to either support the policy as-is, or to explain the problems with the way. This process generally requires focusing on the most important issues unless there is time to discuss the smaller.
